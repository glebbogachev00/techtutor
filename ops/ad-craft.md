# Ad Craft — the story ad

Loaded only for the promotional types. Everything in `voice.md` still applies: no hype,
no em dash, no emoji, no exclamation marks, short sentences, blank lines between beats.

This document adds the direct-response craft on top of that voice — the working methods of
David Ogilvy, Gary Halbert and John Carlton, adapted to a coding school in Vietnam run by a
founder who does not shout. These are techniques restated in our own terms, not their copy.

## The one principle

An ad that reads like an ad gets skipped. An ad that reads like a story gets finished.
Nobody has ever bought from someone who bored them. If the post is dull, no offer inside it
will save it. Being boring is the only unrecoverable mistake.

## Lead with the story. Almost always.

Halbert opened the overwhelming majority of his ads with a story and built the whole piece
around it. Do the same. The story is not decoration in front of the pitch — it *is* the
argument, and the offer is what the story makes obvious.

Order of operations: attention, then interest, then desire, then the ask. In that order,
never jumbled. You cannot ask for anything from someone whose attention you never got.

## The hook: find the detail he thinks is boring

This is Carlton's real lesson, and it is the most useful idea in this document.

His most copied headline came from a client mentioning, almost in passing, that he had
worked out a new golf swing after watching a man with one leg play. To the client that was
old news, barely worth saying. It took nearly an hour of questioning to surface it. That
throwaway detail was worth more than everything the client thought was important.

So: **the best hook is usually the thing Gleb mentioned flatly, as if it were ordinary.**
A teacher deliberately setting work too hard. A kid who spent an hour making a cat say no.
A student who was turned away. He says these plainly because to him they are just Tuesday.
Those are the hooks.

A hook works when the reader cannot help thinking *how is that possible?* or *wait, why
would you do that?* Build it from one shockingly specific detail, never from a category.

- Dead: "Help your child build real skills this summer."
- Dead: "Is your child ready for the future?"
- Alive: "Hung's mother brought him to us to get him off video games. His teacher has spent every lesson since making the work harder."
- Alive: "We turned down eleven students last month and I want to explain why."

If the first line does not make the second line necessary, the first line is wrong. Spend a
disproportionate amount of your effort here; Ogilvy reckoned the headline carried most of the
value of the entire ad, and he was not exaggerating for effect.

## Long copy is allowed here

This is the one place length is permitted. Someone who is interested will read a long post.
Someone who is bored leaves at line two no matter how short it is.

The rule is not "be short." The rule is that every line must earn the next one. Cut whatever
the story does not need, then let the story run as long as it needs. Eight to fifteen short
paragraphs is normal.

## Facts outsell adjectives

Specifics persuade; adjectives are noise. Numbers, names, ages, tools, durations, prices.

- Weak: "Our small classes mean personal attention."
- Strong: "One on one, 60 minutes, over Zoom. The teacher writes software for a living."

Every claim gets a fact attached to it or gets deleted. Halbert's view was that the writer
who researches hardest wins — gather far more concrete material than you can use, then
choose. If a paragraph contains no verifiable specific, it is filler.

## Write to one person

One parent, on a phone, at nine at night, mildly worried about their kid. Not an audience,
not a segment. Use "you" and mean one person. Every sentence should make that one reader
feel understood rather than sold to.

## The offer comes late, and once

Earn it first. The reader should already want it before it appears.

A shape that works:

1. The hook. The specific, slightly baffling detail. No preamble.
2. Hold it for a beat. Do not explain immediately.
3. The story that explains it.
4. The idea the story reveals, developed concretely.
5. The offer, once, in one or two lines.
6. One ask.

Never open with the offer. Never mention it twice. Never stack two asks.

## One price, not a rate card

Quote at most one number, the one the story earns. A post listing every tier, every duration
and every add-on is a price list wearing a story's clothes. See `business-facts.md` for what
is true and for which price fits which story.

## Give the reason why

An unexplained discount reads as desperation or as a lie. A discount with a true reason reads
as an honest business decision, and it converts better. Ogilvy's habit of explaining *why*
applies to every claim, not just discounts.

- Weak: "30% off this month only."
- Strong: "We are opening two evening classes in August and I would rather they start full
  than half empty. Those two are 30% off. The rest are not."

If there is no true reason, do not invent one. State the plain thing instead: what it costs,
what you get.

## Honest scarcity only

State capacity as fact when it is fact. "Four spots left in the Tuesday class" is fine when
four spots are left. "Limited spots, act now" is banned in every case, always.

## Banned in ads specifically

- Opening with the offer or the discount
- Any urgency that is not literally true
- Fake countdowns, fake scarcity, fake "last chance"
- Testimonial-shaped sentences nobody actually said
- Benefits stacked into a list of adjectives
- Any promise about the child's future that cannot be evidenced
- "Invest in your child's future"
- Prices without what they include
- A second ask
