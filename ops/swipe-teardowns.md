# Swipe Teardowns

Mechanics pulled out of the ads in `~/Documents/ads`, each one sorted into **transfers** or
**rejected**, and each transferable one written out as what it looks like for TechTutor.

Deliberately no third-party copy reproduced here. Two reasons. The legal one is obvious. The
practical one matters more: that voice is 1970s American mail-order — loud, agitating, heavy
on capitals and exclamation marks. Imitating it would undo everything in `voice.md`.
**Mechanics transpose across markets. Voice does not.** Take the machine, leave the paint.

---

## 1. The Reversed Constraint — the master pattern

**Where it appears:** Carlton's one-legged golfer ad, and his "from your knees" ad. Both run
the same engine. In the golfer ad the pro literally feels sorry for the man with one leg,
right up until the man drives 320 yards straight down the fairway. The reveal is that being
forced to balance on one leg was not a handicap at all — it was what loaded the swing.

**The mechanic:** find the thing everyone reads as the problem, then show it is the
mechanism. The proof is stronger *because* the constraint is extreme. If it works from your
knees, it will certainly work standing up.

**Why it is the strongest pattern available:** it creates the "how is that possible?"
reaction on its own, with no adjectives doing any work. The curiosity is structural.

**TechTutor transposition — and you already have one:**

Hung's mother brought him in to get him *off* video games. His teacher then spent every
lesson deliberately making the work harder than Hung could comfortably do. The gaming looks
like the problem. It is actually the reason the method lands: games trained him to expect
escalating difficulty, so homework pitched one step past comfortable is the only kind he will
stay with.

Others sitting in the business waiting to be written:

- The kid who cannot sit still through a maths worksheet but spent two hours hunting one bug.
  The short attention span is not the obstacle; it is the signal about what the work should be.
- A child who barely speaks English shipping a working program. The language gap forces them
  to read the code itself rather than the tutorial's prose.
- Turning students away. The constraint on capacity is the proof of the teaching model.

**Rule:** whenever the raw material contains an apparent disadvantage, check whether it is
secretly the mechanism before writing anything else.

## 2. The two-part headline

**Where it appears:** both Carlton ads. A small qualifying line on top, then the big specific
promise underneath. The top line finds the right reader and asks a question they would answer
yes to. The main line delivers the surprising specific.

**Transfers:** the two-part structure, and putting the odd concrete detail in the main line
rather than the benefit.

**Rejected:** the stacked-benefit headline. Carlton's golf headline crams distance, hooks,
slices and strokes into one sentence with an exclamation mark. For a parent choosing a school
that reads as a scam. One promise per headline, no exclamation mark.

**TechTutor version:**

> Parents ask me if their kid is too old to start at 14.
>
> Manthan gives Hung homework he cannot finish. On purpose.

---

## 3. The sub-headline spine

**Where it appears:** every long ad here. Bold sub-headlines every four or five paragraphs,
each one written so it works alone.

**The mechanic:** most people scan before they read. If someone reads only the bold lines
top to bottom, they should still get the whole argument and still be able to act. The ad is
built to be read twice — once at speed, once properly.

**Transfers:** completely, and it is underused in our long posts. A story ad should have two
or three of these, each carrying the story forward on its own.

**Constraint from `voice.md`:** no markdown bold in captions. On Facebook, use a standalone
short line with blank lines around it instead. Same function, no formatting.

---

## 4. Name the mechanism

**Where it appears:** the golf swing is not "a better technique," it is the Triple Coil
Swing. Named, ownable, repeatable.

**The mechanic:** a named thing feels like a real method rather than an opinion. It also
gives you something to refer back to across many posts.

**TechTutor transposition:** you already have **Tech Bash** and you already have **missions**.
What is not named yet is the teaching decision itself — keeping the work one step past
comfortable. Name that and you can build a dozen posts on it. It is the most distinctive
thing about how you teach and right now it is described in a sentence and dropped.

Worth naming. Then `business-facts.md` gets the name and every future post can lean on it.

---

## 5. Credit transfer

**Where it appears:** the narrator insists what he learned had almost nothing to do with him
and everything to do with the expert who taught him.

**The mechanic:** handing the credit away raises the narrator's credibility instead of
lowering it. Someone claiming their own brilliance is resistible; someone crediting a named
colleague is not.

**TechTutor transposition:** this suits you unusually well, because you already write in a
quiet register. Credit Manthan by name. Credit the kid. "Manthan worked out that Hung stops
paying attention when the work is easy" is stronger than "we design our curriculum around
engagement."

---

## 6. Open loops

**Where it appears:** the golfer ad tells you the physics and which leg will be explained
later, then moves on. The reader keeps going to close the loop.

**Transfers, with restraint.** One loop per post, and it must actually close inside the same
post. Never as a teaser for a link.

**Rejected:** withholding as a sales device — the "you'll discover which one on page four"
move. A parent reading a Facebook post will not chase it, and it feels like a trick.

---

## 7. Handle the objection before it is raised

**Where it appears:** the knees ad answers "I'm out of shape, I can't bend over" in the deck
copy, before the reader can raise it.

**The mechanic:** kill the biggest objection early, in the reader's own words, while they are
still deciding whether to keep reading.

**TechTutor transposition:** the real objections are known — too expensive, my kid isn't good
at maths, isn't this just more screen time, my kid is too old, will they actually do the
homework. Any story ad should answer the one nearest objection in the first third, plainly,
conceding whatever is true. This is what `fb_myth_buster` already does; story ads should
borrow it rather than treating it as a separate type.

---

## 8. Specific numbers as texture

**Where it appears:** everywhere, constantly. Twelve years of playing. Several thousand
dollars wasted. Handicap sixteen to seven in four months. Nine minutes. 230 yards.

**The mechanic:** numbers read as evidence even when the reader does not check them. Round
numbers read as estimates; odd ones read as measurements.

**Transfers, hard.** But bounded by our own rule: only numbers that are true and on
`business-facts.md` or in the interview. Never a fabricated statistic. This is the single
place where copying these ads would get us into real trouble, because half their numbers are
almost certainly invented.

---

## 9. Risk reversal

**Where it appears:** "see it free if you like" sits above the headline in the knees ad. The
free trial is not buried in the terms, it is the first thing said.

**TechTutor transposition:** you already have the strongest version of this and you bury it
in the last line. **The trial class is a genuine free look with nothing owed.** Occasionally
that belongs at the very top instead of the bottom.

---

## 10. Halbert's qualifying headline

**Where it appears:** "Unless You Are Rich" — a headline that appears to warn people off,
framed as a restriction rather than an invitation.

**The mechanic:** exclusion is more attractive than welcome. Telling someone this is probably
not for them makes them want to establish that it is.

**Transfers, in a much quieter form.** Honest self-selection reads well to Vietnamese parents
and it is true of your business: you are not the cheap option and not everyone should buy.

- "This is probably not the right fit if you want your kid entertained for an hour a week."
- "We are not the cheapest, and I would rather say that up front."

**Rejected:** the fake-exclusivity version. "Only for serious parents" is an insult wearing a
filter's clothes.

---

## 11. Halbert's long factual runway

**Where it appears:** the water-filter ad spends a very long stretch simply teaching — how
much of the earth's water is salt, how little is accessible — before the product appears.

**The mechanic:** teach long enough and thoroughly enough that the product becomes the
obvious conclusion rather than a pitch. The reader talks themselves into it.

**TechTutor transposition:** this is a genuinely good fit and it is a content type we do not
have. Explaining properly why copying a tutorial video teaches nothing, or why difficulty
level determines whether a kid keeps going, would earn the classes without ever selling them.
Consider a `fb_long_explainer` type: teach for twelve paragraphs, mention the school once.

## 12. Daniel Throssell — the quiet Australian school

His Email Storyteller sales page is the most useful file in the folder for us, because his
register is far closer to ours than Halbert's or Carlton's. No shouting, no agitation, no
machismo. He persuades by being interesting and by being straight.

**The visible cliché kill.** The page opens by starting to type the expected fairy-tale
opener, striking it out mid-word, and replacing it with a plain date. The joke and the
signal are the same gesture: I know what you expected, I am not going to do that.

*Transfers as a principle rather than as a gimmick.* Do not literally strike words out. But
when you catch yourself writing the phrase a reader could have predicted, the correction is
almost always more interesting than the phrase. "Every parent wants the best for their child"
becomes "Hung's mother wanted his evenings back."

**One sentence per paragraph, relentlessly.** His whole page is single-line paragraphs with
air between them. This is already rule 10 in `voice.md`, and it is worth noting that the
practitioner Gleb rates most highly is the most extreme about it. Not a stylistic preference.
Load-bearing.

**Documentary receipts.** He does not describe the email he sent, he reproduces it. The story
is evidenced with the actual artifact.

*Transfers, strongly, and it extends what we already do.* We chase exact words in the
interview. This says go further: show the thing. The actual message Hung's mother sent. The
actual name the kid typed. A screenshot of the four blocks. The artifact beats any description
of the artifact.

**Self-aware narration.** He names his own technique while using it — admits he is teasing,
admits he is selling. Naming the move disarms the suspicion instead of triggering it.

*Transfers in a mild form.* A line like "this is the part where I tell you what it costs" is
more trustworthy than pretending the post is not an ad. Suits your voice. Use once, never
twice.

**Price stated plainly and early, with no flinch.** The number sits near the top of the page.

*Transfers.* This matches the honest-capacity principle we already hold. Hiding the price
implies you are ashamed of it. You are not the cheap option and saying so early filters well.

**The escalating one-line stack.** Four or five short questions in a row, each a variation on
the last, building anticipation.

*Transfers sparingly.* Rhythmically strong, but aimed at a worried parent it can tip into
theatre. At most one such run, at most three lines long.

**Rejected from Throssell:** the multi-day tease structure, in-group flattery of loyal
readers, and ALL-CAPS emphasis. All fine for a copywriter's list of copywriters. Wrong for a
parent deciding where their kid spends Tuesday evening.

**One more thing worth noticing.** He describes his framework as going from "life moment" to
story-based email. That is exactly the pipeline this console implements: the Talk step
captures the life moment, the writer turns it into the post. Good sign the architecture is
right.

## 13. Throssell's Grand Unified Theory — the most important idea in the folder

From *Adventures in Copyland* #6. This one corrected a mistake in our own prompt, so it is
worth stating carefully.

**The claim:** the better your story, the worse your sales pitch. Almost always.

**Why:** a good story requires resolution. Resolution leaves the reader satisfied, and thinking
about the story. A good pitch leaves the reader unsatisfied, and thinking about themselves.
Those two endings do not live in the same place. A reader who feels resolved wants nothing.

He demonstrates it on his own work — an email of his that readers praised as a great sales
email, which he says was terrible at selling, because all the selling happened in a 54-word
postscript bolted on after the story had already closed.

**The three jobs a story can do:**

**Job A — demonstrate storytelling skill.** Tell a beautiful story, then say "this is how well
I write, buy my course on writing." *Not available to us.* It only works when the product IS
storytelling. He is blunt that this is his permanent cheat code and that copywriters who imitate
his style to sell other things wonder why their copy bombs. Directly relevant warning for us: we
sell classes for children, so a lovely story will not sell one on its own merit.

**Job B — entertain, engage and bond.** The story does no selling. It exists so the reader
enjoys it, warms to us, and trusts us later. He admits the transitions from these stories to
the pitch are clunky, usually bridged by a "parallel idea," and carry no real sales force.
*This is what most of our posts should be, and what they already are.* Correct for laugh,
proof, curious and teach.

**Job C — sales inception.** The one way a story actually sells:

1. Name the benefit you are selling on.
2. Work out what the reader must BELIEVE to want that benefit.
3. Tell the story whose implicit moral installs that belief.

His textbook example is the old Wall Street Journal two-men letter, whose moral is that some
mysterious factor beyond education and ambition decides a career — a belief the product then
answers.

Suited genres: the transformation story (someone like the reader's child, changed), the identity
story (a life the reader wants), and the cautionary tale. **We use the cautionary tale gently or
not at all** — frightening a parent about their own child is the line we do not cross.

**The seam this exposed in our own rules.** Our anti-slop rule 5 bans morals, lessons and
takeaways. Job C requires a moral. Those look contradictory and are not: the moral must be
**engineered into the choice and shape of the story, and never written down.** The reader
supplies it. A stated moral destroys the effect it was reaching for. That distinction is now
written into the prompt at both ends.

**What it changed for us.** Our Hung draft ended with "his mother relaxed" — a genuinely good
story ending, and by this theory a bad sales ending, because it discharges the desire it built.
For promotional posts the close now has to leave the parent looking at their own kid rather
than at Hung.

**Also worth noting:** he argues email storytelling is not the same craft as fiction, and that
reading novels will not make your sales stories better, because novelists are not selling. Ties
back to teardown 1 — mechanics transpose, forms do not.

## Rejected outright

These recur throughout the swipe file and every one of them would damage trust with a parent
choosing where their child spends their evenings:

- Exclamation marks in bulk, ALL-CAPS shouting, italics-plus-bold on every third phrase
- Agitating insecurity — the reader as a failure who is being laughed at
- Competitive machismo ("slaughtering" players half your age)
- Fabricated or unverifiable numbers and results
- Testimonial-shaped sentences nobody actually said
- Stacked P.S. blocks piling on bonuses
- Fake deadlines, fake stock limits, "act now"
- Anything implying the reader is a bad parent for not buying

The last one is the important line. These ads frequently work by making the reader feel
inadequate. We do the opposite: the parent is treated as intelligent and their doubt is
treated as reasonable. Any mechanic that needs the reader to feel small is not available to
us, however well it converts.
