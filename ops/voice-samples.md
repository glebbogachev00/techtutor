# Voice Samples — the target texture

> **READ THIS BEFORE USING THE SAMPLES BELOW.**
>
> These samples are approved for their VOICE, their honesty, and their use of concrete detail.
> They are NOT approved for their rhythm, and you must not copy their line breaking.
>
> They were written under an older rule that said one idea per line with a blank line after
> every beat. That rule has been removed, because followed literally it produces short
> sentence, full stop, short sentence, full stop, which is now the clearest sign that a machine
> wrote something. Gleb has rejected drafts for exactly this.
>
> So: take the register, the concreteness and the refusal to hype from these samples. Take the
> paragraph rhythm from the human-texture rules instead — longer sentences organised with
> commas and clauses, real paragraphs of three to five sentences, and line breaks only where a
> break actually means something.

These are posts Gleb wrote or approved. They are here as **texture reference only**.

The writer reads these to learn the rhythm: how long the sentences are, where the
blank lines fall, how a moment gets told without being explained. Never reuse the
stories, the names, the phrasings, or the closing lines. If a draft echoes one of
these, it has failed.

---

## SAMPLE 1 — first GDevelop lesson (IG / FB)

> It was their first GDevelop lesson.
>
> The task was a simple platformer level. Grab a chicken. Bring it back to a character. Get a key. Open a door. Move to the next level.
>
> The brother was typing the project name. He said it out loud while he typed.
>
> "Harry Bum Cheeks."
>
> I laughed. The whole class laughed.
>
> He looked up and said, "Yep."
>
> Then he turned back to the computer and kept building.
>
> By the end of the session they had a working level. The chicken moved. The character gave the key. The door opened. The next level loaded.
>
> The name stayed. The project worked.
>
> That is usually how it goes.
>
> Trial class link in comments. What would your kid name their first game?

**What makes it work:** the four-word task sentences in a row, then a long one. The
quote is the exact thing the kid said, not a cleaned-up version. Nothing explains why
the moment mattered. The last line before the CTA is five words and does all the lifting.

## SAMPLE 2 — daughter finishes the game in Python (FB)

> Your daughter gets home and opens her laptop to finish the game she started in class.
>
> This week she's rewriting it in Python, because Scratch got too easy.
>
> That's the part most programs miss. "Coding for kids" usually means watching a video and copying the answer.
>
> At TechTutor the kids build things a real person can use: a Scratch game for a younger sibling, an app that reminds them to drink water, a small site for the family shop.
>
> We keep classes small, and our teachers ship software for a living.
>
> If you've ever wondered what your kid could make if someone took them seriously, bring them to a trial class. Link in the comments.

**What makes it work:** it opens inside a scene, not on a claim. The three examples are
specific objects, not a slogan triad. The offer arrives after the proof, in one sentence.

## SAMPLE 3 — the maths objection (FB)

> Parents ask me this often: my child isn't strong at maths, can they still learn to code?
>
> It's a fair question. Code looks like equations, so the assumption makes sense.
>
> But maths and programming use different parts of the brain.
>
> Khoa, 9, struggled with every maths worksheet his teacher handed him. He also spent two hours one afternoon hunting down a bug in his own game, with nobody asking him to.
>
> The difference was that he wanted the game to work.
>
> Coding teaches you to solve problems, not to calculate.
>
> Come try a trial class and see for yourself. Link in comments.

**What makes it work:** the doubt is stated fairly and in the parent's own words before
it gets answered. One named kid carries the whole argument.

## SAMPLE 5 — Reddit (EN, no brand voice)

> I run a small coding school for 7-17s in Vietnam. A 9yo spent three classes convinced
> her game was broken. It wasn't. She'd flipped two coordinates. Here's the actual bug
> and what it taught her about debugging.

**What makes it work:** credentials in eight words, then straight into the specific
technical thing. No CTA. Reddit smells promotion and punishes it.

## SAMPLE 6 — story ad, long copy (FB)

The raw material: Hung, 12, plays a lot of video games. His mother brought him to us to get
those hours back. After two or three lessons she messaged asking whether he was getting any
homework, because she wasn't seeing him study. His teacher Manthan had been setting homework
every class, deliberately pitched slightly above Hung's level. She relaxed when she heard.

> Hung's mother brought him to us to get him off video games.
>
> His teacher has spent every lesson since making the work harder than Hung can comfortably do.
>
> That was deliberate. It is also the reason it worked.
>
> Two or three lessons in, she messaged us. She wasn't seeing him study at home, so she asked
> straight out: is he getting any homework?
>
> He was. Every class. Manthan had been setting it and Hung had been doing it.
>
> The part she didn't know was how it was pitched. Manthan keeps the exercises a step above
> where Hung actually is. When the work gets too easy, Hung stops paying attention.
>
> Which is exactly what his games had already taught him to expect. A game that stops getting
> harder is a game you put down.
>
> So the homework is built the same way. It runs on Tech Bash, our own platform, as missions.
> The first one is printing a single line of text on the screen. Then imports. Then Python that
> would make an adult stop and read it twice.
>
> His mother now gets the lesson recordings and his marked homework, so she can see the whole
> thing instead of asking. That's our Academic track, 2 million on top of the base plan.
>
> Bring your kid to a trial class. Lessons are one on one over Zoom, 60 minutes.
>
> Link in the comments.

**What makes it work, and what the flat version got wrong:**

The flat version opened "Hung is twelve. He plays a lot of video games" and then walked
forward through the week in the order it happened. That is a report, not an ad. It also
mentioned the video games in line two and never came back to them.

This version leads with the contradiction — a mother wanting less gaming, a teacher
deliberately making things harder — and holds it for three short lines before explaining.
The gaming detail pays off in the middle, because escalating difficulty is the actual link
between the thing she wanted him to stop doing and the thing we do. That link is the one
interesting idea in the material, and it is what earns the offer.

One price, not a rate card. The flat version quoted the base plan, the two-kid variant, the
lesson count, the duration and the Academic add-on: five numbers, none of them load-bearing.
This one quotes the Academic add-on only, because the story is about a mother who wanted
visibility, and drops the delivery facts into the last two lines where they belong.

## Counter-samples — this is the slop we are avoiding

> 🚀 Khóa học coding đột phá — giúp con bạn trở thành creator thực thụ! Đăng ký ngay hôm nay để unlock tiềm năng! 🔥

> We're excited to announce our world-class coding program that empowers the next
> generation of young innovators! Ask me anything!

And the subtler slop, which is the real enemy, because it does not look like slop:

> Here's the thing about kids and coding.
>
> It's not just about learning to type commands. It's about learning to think.
>
> And that's when it clicked for me: the magic isn't in the code. It's in the confidence.
>
> Every child deserves that moment.

Nothing in that last one is false. It is still unusable. There is no kid in it, no day,
no object, no thing that happened. It is four rhetorical structures stacked on each
other: "it's not just X, it's Y", "that's when it clicked", a colon-reveal, and a
universal closer. Those four moves are the fingerprint. Never make them.
